# Part 1 — The three EM funds

## First, the mechanical fact that changes the China argument completely

The brief you wrote said the EM funds "DO de-THB in economic-exposure terms even though they sit on a Thai platform." For India and Vietnam that is arguable. **For China it is simply false**, and the reason is worth understanding properly because it is the load-bearing fact in the biggest sell.

SCBCEH is a **feeder fund** — a Thai-registered fund that does not pick stocks itself, but takes your baht and buys units in a foreign master fund. You give the manager baht; the manager converts to dollars or Hong Kong dollars; the master fund holds Chinese equities.

Now, in the ordinary case, that gives you two exposures at once, and this is the part that matters:

1. **Asset exposure** — how Chinese stocks perform.
2. **Currency exposure** — how the Hong Kong dollar (pegged to USD) moves against the baht.

If the baht weakens from 32.77 to 36, an *unhedged* feeder fund gains about 10% in baht terms before the underlying stocks do anything at all. That currency leg is the entire reason a baht-based investor buys foreign assets as a de-THB move. The stocks are the point, but the currency leg is what actually reduces your dependence on the baht.

**SCBCEH deliberately removes leg 2.** Its full registered name at the Thai SEC (project ID `M0155_2556`) is **"SCB CHINA EQUITY THB HEDGE"**, and it hedges 95–105% of its currency exposure back to baht. Mechanically, the manager sells the foreign currency forward — enters a contract to convert the foreign currency back to baht at a rate fixed today. So whatever the currency does, your baht outcome is unchanged.

The result: **you own the Chinese stock risk and none of the currency benefit.** If the baht collapses, SCBCEH does not protect you. It is, in the one dimension your entire mission is about, a baht asset wearing a Chinese costume.

**Status: Verified.** Two independent sources agree. The Thai SEC registry name, and your own repo — `apps/investment-analyst-agents/src/briefing/briefing-agent.ts:7` has said `"currency hedged to THB"` in `FUND_METADATA` since the file was written. The fact was sitting in your codebase the whole time. What the same line got *wrong* is the underlying: it says "China A-share & Hong Kong equity." Sentinel says it is **HSCEI** — the Hang Seng China Enterprises index, which is Hong Kong-listed Chinese companies only, heavy in state banks and energy, with near-zero mainland A-share weight. That inverts the sanctions read, because Hong Kong-listed shares clear through the international dollar system in a way mainland A-shares do not.

There is also a smaller point worth noting: hedging is not free. Maintaining a 95–105% forward hedge costs roughly the interest-rate difference between the two currencies, deducted from your return every year, in perpetuity. You have been paying an ongoing fee to *remove* the exact exposure you are trying to acquire.

**For India and Vietnam the position is different and weaker.** Neither fund is hedged *by name*, so both probably do give you the currency leg. Whether they hedge at manager discretion is **unverified** — nobody checked the prospectuses. This does not rescue India, for reasons below, but it is an honest asymmetry: the China case is airtight and the India case is not.

# Part 2 — Why the deployment pace should not change

This is the section where the gap between a real number and a decision-relevant number is largest, so let me build it step by step.

## Step 1 — Ledger's bleed figure is exactly right

Your idle baht cash is ฿500,000 in SAVINGS_THB plus ฿361,223.50 in CASH_THB = **฿861,223.50**. Dime Save pays about 1%. Thai inflation is about 2.8%. The **real yield** — the nominal rate minus inflation, meaning what your purchasing power actually does — is therefore about **−1.8%**.

฿861,223.50 × 1.8% = **฿15,502 per year.**

That is a genuine, correctly computed annual loss of purchasing power. Ledger is right, and his second point is right too: baht cash is not neutral for you. Baht cash *is* Thai exposure. Holding ฿861k in baht while running a de-THB mission is not a neutral parking spot; it is the mission working against itself.

## Step 2 — but ฿15,502 is not what you are deciding

Here is the move that gets lost. **฿15,502 is the cost of holding cash at all. It is not the cost of holding cash for one month longer than some alternative schedule.**

You are not choosing between "deploy" and "never deploy." You already decided to deploy. The existing plan puts roughly ฿175,000 per month into DIME over about five months. The ฿861k goes to zero either way. Ledger's proposal is ฿190,000 per month over four months instead.

So the only thing being decided is **when, within a five-month window, some of the money moves.** The ฿15,502 figure attaches to a balance that is already scheduled to disappear.

## Step 3 — the actual arithmetic

Take the ฿916,000 deployable and compute the weighted-average month each baht leaves cash.

**Plan A, ฿175,000/month:** ฿175k in months 1 through 5, remainder in month 5. Weighted-average deployment point = **month 3.13**.

**Plan B, ฿190,000/month:** ฿190k in months 1 through 4, remainder after. Weighted-average deployment point = **month 2.93**.

Difference: **0.21 months — about 6 days earlier, on average, across the whole balance.**

Now value those six days at the real-yield gap:

฿916,000 × 1.8% × (0.21 ÷ 12) = **฿287**

Different reasonable schedule assumptions move this around; Vizier's version came out at **฿225**. The exact figure depends on how you handle the final month's remainder, and that is the honest answer — it is somewhere in the low hundreds of baht, and no assumption you can defend gets it much higher.

Here is a bound that needs no schedule assumption at all. Suppose acceleration advanced **every single baht by a full month** — far more than Ledger proposes:

฿916,000 × 1.8% ÷ 12 = **฿1,374**

That is the theoretical ceiling on the entire acceleration decision. The realistic value is about a fifth of it.

## Step 4 — the gap, stated plainly

**฿15,502 is the cost of the cash. ฿287 is the value of the decision.** The ratio is about 54 to 1. The bleed figure is real and it is being used to argue for a change that captures roughly 1.9% of it.

Against that ฿287 you are putting: one month of additional market-timing risk on ฿15,000 per month, immediately in front of FOMC on 16 September and the Bank of Japan on 17 September, back to back. A single 2% adverse move on the accelerated portion costs more than ฿287. The expected value of the change is somewhere near zero and the variance is much larger than the edge.

## The 10.03% figure, and the correction underneath it

Why two numbers for one holding? Not for the reason you might guess:

| Method | Denominator | Result |
|---|---|---|

# Part 5 — KBank staff dealing rules: the finding nobody costed

## What is actually verified

- **KBank publishes a Code of Conduct** binding on directors and employees, covering the use of inside information in securities and derivatives trading. **Verified that it exists and covers this subject.** Its provisions are unread — kasikornbank.com returned 403.
- **The Securities and Exchange Act B.E. 2535 insider-trading prohibition** binds any person holding material non-public information. This applies to you from day one regardless of any internal policy. **Verified.**
- **SEC Notification Sor Jor. 6/2567** (effective 16 March 2024) governs reporting of holdings changes — but this is the **Form 59 regime for directors and executives** and their spouses and minor children, not general staff. **Plausible inference: as an incoming MSBA hire you will not be a Form 59 reporter.** Sentinel raised this specifically to rule it out before it caused confusion.
- **Thai SEC conduct-of-business rules require licensed intermediaries** — securities companies and asset managers — to maintain staff personal-dealing controls. **This is the pivotal unknown.** KSecurities and KAsset are intermediaries; the commercial bank itself is not, in the same way. **Which entity and which unit you join materially changes the answer.**

## What a typical Thai bank policy contains

This list is sector-standard expectation, **not** KBank policy:

- **Pre-clearance** — you must request approval before placing an order, and approval is valid only briefly, commonly same-day to about 48 hours.
- **A restricted list** — names where the bank holds material non-public information from lending or advisory work. Usually predominantly Thai issuers. Often accompanied by an undisclosed **watch list**.
- **A minimum holding period** — commonly around 30 days, to prevent staff day-trading.
- **Blackout windows** around KBANK's own results.
- **Declaration of all brokerage accounts** on joining, typically within 30 days, sometimes with forced divestment of restricted names.
- **Scope covering all securities including foreign**, with a carve-out for diversified funds and broad-based index ETFs.

## What this does to your plan, ranked by damage

**1. The USD dip-ladder — highest break risk, and this is structural rather than a matter of degree.**

Your ladder is built on resting triggers: VOO ≤ 705, SMH ≤ 560, LLY ≤ 1080, UBER ≤ 65, GLDM ≤ 79. The design intent is that the order fires **the moment the level is hit**. That is precisely what a pre-clearance regime forbids. Approval must be obtained *before* the order and lasts only a short window. A good-till-cancelled limit order placed weeks in advance cannot be pre-cleared in any coherent way — at the moment you would need approval, there is no order to approve; at the moment the order executes, the approval has expired.

**The remediation is cheap, and it is worth doing now rather than in December.** Convert the ladder from auto-fire to a three-step flow: **alert fires → request clearance → execute inside the approval window.** Same price levels, same thesis, same tranche sizes. Different plumbing. The only thing you lose is execution immediacy, which for a dip ladder measured in whole percentage points is a minor cost.
